# Event: The Worst Student in the Best Study Group

**NPC:** molly  
**Event ID:** `molly_worst_student_best_group`  
**Trigger:** Day > 8
**Image:** large (Academy library study table with Molly, Emma, cards, and notes)  
**Emotion:** embarrassed

## Text

Molly has commandeered a table in the Academy library and fortified it with books, practice cards, three cups of tea, and a plate of biscuits. She has also recruited Emma and you to help her prepare for the upcoming artificery examination. The biscuits, at least, suggest a sound understanding of the principles of successful collaboration.

"Thank you both so much!" she says as you sit down. "I really have been studying, but everything becomes muddled when the professors ask me to explain it. I thought perhaps if we worked together..."

"Naturally," Emma replies. She draws a neat diagram in Molly's notebook. "The central distinction is between essence affinity and the allocation of a card's expressive capacity. Once you've accounted for the constraints on manifestation, the rest follows."

Molly considers this in silence. "Yes," she says brightly. "Of course."

Emma advances to a second diagram. It has more arrows. "Now, in the case of a composite effect—"

"Sorry," Molly interrupts softly. "Could we perhaps go back to the first arrow?"

Emma looks down at her page. "Which first arrow?"

"The one before all the other arrows."

You watch Emma take a slow breath. For a young woman capable of explaining a complex enchantment in four elegant sentences, the discovery that four sentences might not be enough is proving unexpectedly educational.

Molly notices, too. Her smile thins. "I'm sorry. You're both being so patient. I think I must be the worst student in the best study group at the Academy."

"That's not what I—" Emma begins, then stops. Her expression softens. "Molly, I know you're trying."

"I know." Molly looks at her notes. "I just wish trying felt a little more like understanding."

Emma sets down her pen. The three of you have an evening, a pile of practice cards, and rather more work to do than the biscuits were prepared for. You can approach the problem in several ways.

## Options

### Option A: Start over with ordinary things

**Text:**

"All right," you say, putting Emma's diagram aside. "Forget expressive capacity for a moment. Imagine you're growing a garden."

"Oh!" Molly sits up. "I can imagine that."

You use seeds to represent captured essences, garden beds to represent a card's limited capacity, and different plants to show how the same resources produce different results depending on their arrangement. Molly begins asking questions without apologizing first. Soon she is correcting one of your examples: "But those two plants won't grow happily together. They compete for the same soil. Wouldn't that be like putting incompatible effects on one card?"

Emma blinks. "Actually, yes. Precisely."

"Oh." Molly looks delighted and astonished in equal measure. "I understood one!"

"More than one," you point out. "You just explained the next problem."

By the end of the session Molly has drawn a garden across half a notebook. Its flowers are annotated with surprisingly accurate artificery principles. Emma studies the page and admits, with graceful reluctance, that the method is effective.

As you gather your things, Molly presses two small moxes into your hand. "These were spare practice materials. I think you'll make better use of them than I would tonight. And I'd like to thank you for finding a way to explain things that made sense to me."

### Option B: Practice the game, one decision at a time

**Text:**

You clear the diagrams away and deal a small practice hand. "Let's forget the examination wording. You've got these cards, this much mana, and an opponent about to attack. What's your move?"

Molly relaxes visibly. "Oh, I know this one. I think."

The first round goes badly. Molly spends her resources too early and leaves her best defender exposed. You reset the position and ask what she would change. On the second attempt she keeps a card in reserve. On the third she spots an unfavorable trade before Emma can point it out.

Emma leans forward, interested despite herself. "Good. Now suppose your opponent is bluffing. What information do you actually have?"

"Not enough," Molly says after a moment. "So I shouldn't bet everything on being right."

Emma smiles. "Exactly."

You work through a dozen little encounters, each illustrating one principle. Emma gradually abandons her prepared lecture and begins inventing increasingly devious positions for Molly to solve. The two of them are laughing by the time Molly wins a practice exchange with a move neither of you suggested.

"There!" Molly announces, triumphantly placing her final card. "I did it myself!"

"And made the correct choice for the correct reason," Emma replies. She turns to you. "A much better teaching method than another hour of definitions, Fibonacci. Nicely judged."

High praise from Emma. You resist the temptation to have the words engraved on a plaque.

### Option C: Show Molly what she's already good at

**Text:**

You look at the scattered cards. One of them depicts a creature of the forest; Molly has absentmindedly placed it beside a green incantation and arranged the pair so their effects complement one another.

"Molly," you say, "I've noticed something. Whenever a lesson involves plants, animals, or growth, you stop worrying about the terminology and start seeing how everything connects. You've got a real knack for green essence."

She looks up sharply. "Me?"

"Yes, you. You know what makes living things thrive. You notice details the rest of us miss. That's exactly what this kind of artificery needs."

Molly stares at you. She opens her mouth, closes it, and looks down at the cards again. "I've spent so much time trying to be as good at everything as Emma," she says quietly. "I didn't think there was something I might be particularly good at myself."

Emma reaches for the cards and examines Molly's arrangement. "Antonio is right. I wouldn't have chosen that combination, but it's coherent—and rather clever. I may have been teaching you the subject I find easiest instead of the part that suits you."

"You really think so?" Molly asks.

"I do," Emma says. "Try explaining _your_ choice to us."

And Molly does. Hesitantly at first, then with the eager confidence she brings to a conversation about a new trail or an injured animal. She shows you how the growth effects support the creature rather than simply making it stronger. Following her reasoning, you notice a connection in green artificery that you had previously overlooked.

When she finishes, Molly gives you a long, grateful look. "Antonio... thank you. You made me feel as though I might actually belong here."

For once, her smile is almost shy. Emma quietly pushes the plate of biscuits toward the middle of the table, allowing the moment to remain Molly's.
